Northeastern State University, located in the heart of Cherokee Nation's tribal jurisdiction, has expanded its collaboration with the tribe's language preservation programs, giving students more structured opportunities to study Cherokee language and culture alongside tribal educators and researchers. The university has long maintained a relationship with Cherokee Nation given its Tahlequah location, but recent additions to coursework and research partnerships mark a deeper level of institutional coordination.

University faculty describe the expanded collaboration as recognition that Cherokee Nation's own language revitalization infrastructure, including its immersion schools and archival work, represents an educational resource that a nearby public university is well positioned to support rather than duplicate.

## Coursework Rooted in Tribal Partnership

New and expanded course sections give students access to instruction developed in coordination with Cherokee Nation's language program staff, rather than relying solely on university faculty without direct tribal program involvement. The arrangement has meant closer scheduling coordination between the university and tribal educators, some of whom split time between Cherokee Nation programs and university teaching responsibilities.

Students enrolled in the expanded track gain exposure not only to language instruction but to the broader context of Cherokee Nation's revitalization efforts, including the challenges of teaching a language with a declining number of first-language speakers and the tribe's long-term strategy for reversing that trend through immersion education starting in early childhood.

## Research Opportunities Tied to Preservation Work

Beyond coursework, the university has expanded opportunities for students to participate in archival and research projects connected to Cherokee Nation's language preservation initiatives, including work supporting documentation of oral histories and educational materials development. Faculty say these research placements give students practical experience while contributing directly to tribal priorities, rather than treating the tribe primarily as a subject of academic study.

University administrators emphasize that the expanded partnership was shaped substantially by input from Cherokee Nation itself, reflecting an approach to tribal collaboration that treats the tribe as an active partner in defining the scope and direction of joint programming rather than a passive subject of external research interest.

The university expects continued growth in student interest in the program, particularly among students from Cherokee Nation communities themselves who see the coursework as connected to family and community history. Program leaders say future expansion will depend on continued coordination with tribal partners and on sustained interest from students across a range of academic backgrounds, not solely those pursuing degrees explicitly focused on Native American studies.
