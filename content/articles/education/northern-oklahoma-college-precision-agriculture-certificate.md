Northern Oklahoma College has introduced a new precision agriculture certificate, a short-term credential aimed at helping students master the GPS-guided equipment and farm management software that have become standard tools on wheat and cattle operations across northern Oklahoma.

The certificate can be completed in roughly a year and is designed for students who may already be working part-time on a family farm or ranch and want formal training in the technology increasingly built into modern equipment, from auto-steer tractor systems to soil-sampling software that helps farmers decide where to apply fertilizer most efficiently.

## Technology Reshaping a Traditional Industry

Farm equipment dealers in the region have said for years that the technical skill required to operate and troubleshoot modern machinery has grown well beyond what earlier generations needed to know, even as the basic work of planting, tending, and harvesting a wheat crop has remained recognizably the same. Precision agriculture tools promise real savings on fuel, seed, and fertilizer, but only when operators know how to set up and interpret the data the equipment produces.

College instructors involved in building the certificate say much of the coursework is intentionally hands-on, using donated and loaner equipment from area dealerships so students get time behind the controls of the same machines they're likely to encounter on a real operation, rather than learning the concepts purely in a classroom setting.

## A Credential Built Around Local Needs

Unlike a traditional two-year associate degree, the certificate is structured to let students move quickly into the workforce, or back onto a family operation, without committing to years of additional coursework unrelated to their immediate goals. College officials describe the program as filling a gap between traditional vocational agriculture classes offered in area high schools and full four-year agricultural engineering degrees offered at land-grant universities elsewhere in the state.

Early enrollment has drawn a mix of traditional-age students and older learners already working in agriculture who want to formalize skills they've picked up informally. Northern Oklahoma College officials say they plan to track how many certificate completers go on to work directly in agriculture, either on family operations or with equipment dealers and agronomy service providers, as they consider whether to expand the program's course offerings in future years.
