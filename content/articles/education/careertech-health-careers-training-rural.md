Oklahoma's statewide CareerTech system has expanded health careers training offerings at technology centers serving rural counties in the northern part of the state, part of a broader effort to build a stronger local talent pipeline for hospitals and clinics that have struggled to recruit staff from outside their immediate communities. The expanded programming spans certified nursing assistant training, medical office administration, and emergency medical technician coursework.

Technology center administrators say the expansion reflects years of feedback from rural healthcare employers, who have consistently reported difficulty attracting workers willing to relocate to smaller communities. Training local residents already rooted in the area, rather than trying to recruit from larger cities, has become a more central strategy for addressing chronic staffing gaps.

## Meeting Employers Where They Are

CareerTech instructors have worked directly with area hospitals and clinics to align coursework with the specific certifications and competencies those employers need, rather than offering a generic health careers curriculum disconnected from local hiring realities. In several cases, technology centers have arranged clinical rotation placements directly with partner healthcare facilities, giving students practical experience alongside their classroom instruction.

Program leaders emphasize that many students entering these tracks are adults already working in other fields who see healthcare as a stable, in-demand career path within reach of their home community. The programs are structured to accommodate those students, with flexible scheduling options for people balancing coursework with existing jobs or family responsibilities.

## A Long-Term Bet on Local Talent

Rural hospital administrators have described local training pipelines as a more sustainable long-term solution to staffing shortages than repeated recruitment campaigns aimed at drawing workers from larger cities, an approach that has had limited success given competitive pay and lifestyle differences. By contrast, students trained through nearby CareerTech centers often have existing family and community ties that make them more likely to stay in the area after completing certification.

Technology center leaders say enrollment in the expanded health careers tracks has grown steadily, though they caution that the ultimate measure of success will be how many graduates end up working in local healthcare facilities rather than moving elsewhere after certification. Employers have offered informal commitments to prioritize hiring from these programs, though staffing decisions ultimately rest with each individual facility.

Looking forward, CareerTech administrators say they plan to continue expanding health careers offerings at additional rural technology centers, contingent on continued interest from both students and local healthcare employers willing to partner on clinical placements and curriculum development.
