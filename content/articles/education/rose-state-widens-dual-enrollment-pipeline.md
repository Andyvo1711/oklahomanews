Rose State College has added seven new dual-enrollment partnerships with high schools across Midwest City, Del City, and eastern Oklahoma City this spring, part of a broader push to let more metro-area seniors leave high school with a semester or more of college credit already banked.

The expansion follows several years of steady growth in the college's Concurrent Enrollment program, which allows qualifying juniors and seniors to take Rose State courses either on campus, online, or taught by credentialed high school teachers inside their home school building. Tuition for concurrent courses is discounted well below the standard credit-hour rate, a detail administrators say matters most for families who would otherwise rule out a four-year path entirely.

## Reducing the First-Semester Shock

College advisors describe the first semester of freshman year as the point where many first-generation students quietly disappear from enrollment rolls, overwhelmed by unfamiliar course loads and the sudden cost of tuition, books, and fees. Dual-enrollment credit doesn't just save money; it gives students an early, lower-stakes look at college-level expectations while a familiar high school teacher and counselor are still nearby to help.

Rose State's model leans on partnerships with counselors at the sending high schools, who help identify students who are performing well but may not otherwise think of themselves as "college material." Outreach staff visit participating schools several times each semester to walk families through the paperwork, financial aid implications, and how banked credit transfers toward an associate degree or toward transfer to a four-year university.

## Scaling Without Losing Rigor

The tricky part of any expansion, faculty say, is keeping the courses at genuine college rigor even as more sections open in more buildings. Rose State requires concurrent-enrollment instructors to meet the same credentialing standards as on-campus adjunct faculty, and the college conducts periodic classroom observations to confirm that syllabi, grading, and assignments match the on-campus version of each course.

Early data the college has tracked internally suggests that students who complete at least two dual-enrollment courses in high school are more likely to enroll in college the following fall and to persist into a second year, though administrators are careful to note that correlation is not the same as proof the program alone drives the outcome. Still, the trend has been strong enough to justify the additional advising staff and course sections added this year.

For families in Midwest City and Del City, the practical effect is straightforward: a student who completes a full slate of concurrent courses across junior and senior year could realistically enter Rose State, or transfer on to a four-year school, with a full semester of general education requirements already satisfied. College leaders say that head start, more than any single scholarship, is what tends to change a family's calculation about whether college is affordable at all.
