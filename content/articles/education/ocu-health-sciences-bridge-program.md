Oklahoma City University has introduced a bridge program designed for students whose undergraduate degrees did not include the science coursework typically expected of applicants to health sciences graduate programs. The one-year track is aimed at career-changers and students who discovered an interest in healthcare fields later in their academic journey, giving them a structured path to catch up on prerequisite coursework.

University administrators say the program responds to a pattern they had observed for years: students with strong academic records in unrelated fields, from business to the humanities, who wanted to pursue nursing, physician assistant studies, or other clinical graduate programs but lacked the foundational science credits required for admission.

## Structured Coursework, Compressed Timeline

The bridge program compresses core science prerequisites, including anatomy, physiology, and chemistry, into a sequence designed to be completed within a single academic year rather than spread across multiple years of part-time coursework. Faculty say the compressed format requires significant commitment from students but offers a clearer, faster path than piecing together prerequisites individually at a community college or through scattered continuing education courses.

Advisors work closely with bridge program students to identify which graduate programs they are targeting, since specific prerequisite requirements vary across nursing, physician assistant, and other clinical training programs. That individualized advising, university officials say, is central to the program's design, since a one-size-fits-all prerequisite sequence would not serve students aiming for different graduate destinations.

## Addressing Oklahoma's Healthcare Workforce Needs

The timing of the program's launch reflects broader workforce pressures facing Oklahoma's healthcare system, particularly in nursing and allied health fields where demand has outpaced the number of graduates entering the workforce each year. University leaders say expanding the pool of qualified graduate program applicants, including those who arrive via non-traditional academic paths, is one piece of addressing that gap.

Early cohorts of the program have included former teachers, business professionals, and recent liberal arts graduates, a mix that university officials say reflects genuine diversity in when and how people decide to pursue healthcare careers. Faculty note that these students often bring valuable communication and interpersonal skills developed in prior careers, qualities considered important in clinical training even though they are not typically taught in prerequisite science courses.

The university plans to evaluate the program's outcomes as its first full cohort completes coursework and applies to graduate programs, with particular attention to how well graduates perform in competitive admissions processes compared to students who completed prerequisites through more traditional routes. Program leaders describe the initiative as one part of a broader institutional effort to diversify pathways into Oklahoma's health sciences workforce.
