The University of Tulsa has spent years building a reputation in cybersecurity education, and it is now directing more of that expertise toward a sector deeply woven into the local economy: energy. Faculty in the university's computer science and cyber programs have expanded course offerings that address the specific vulnerabilities facing pipeline operators, refiners, and grid managers, many of whom are headquartered in or near Tulsa.

The shift responds to a broader industry recognition that energy infrastructure, much of it built decades before digital connectivity became standard, faces cybersecurity challenges distinct from those in traditional information technology environments. Protecting industrial control systems requires different technical grounding than securing a corporate email server, and university faculty say the curriculum update reflects that distinction.

## Coursework Tailored to Industrial Systems

New course sections walk students through the architecture of operational technology systems, the kind that control physical processes like pipeline flow or refinery operations, as distinct from the standard networked computing environments covered in most cybersecurity programs. Students work through simulated scenarios involving legacy equipment, a common feature of energy infrastructure that was not originally designed with modern network security in mind.

Faculty overseeing the changes say the goal is not to replace foundational cybersecurity training but to layer industry-specific context on top of it, giving graduates a head start when they move into roles at energy companies that maintain both traditional IT systems and specialized industrial networks side by side.

## A Talent Pipeline Rooted in Tulsa

Tulsa's economy has long included a significant concentration of energy sector employers, from midstream pipeline operators to companies involved in exploration and production support services. University leaders describe the expanded cybersecurity focus as a way to strengthen that relationship further, positioning local graduates as a ready talent pool for an industry that has increasingly prioritized digital security investment.

The program has also drawn interest from students who might not have previously considered energy as a career path, given the sector's reputation among some young professionals as less technology-forward than software or finance. Faculty say the coursework helps counter that perception by demonstrating how much of modern energy operation now depends on complex digital systems requiring skilled protection.

University administrators expect the expanded curriculum to continue evolving as energy companies clarify their specific hiring needs. In the meantime, the program is positioning itself as a regional resource, not just for traditional four-year students but potentially for working professionals at area energy companies seeking additional cybersecurity training relevant to their specific operational environments. Program leaders say further expansion will depend on continued collaboration with industry partners willing to share the technical specifics of the challenges they face.
