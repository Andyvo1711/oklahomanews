Cherokee Nation's economic development arm has expanded a grant program supporting tribal citizen-owned small businesses across eastern Oklahoma, part of a broader effort to grow economic activity within the tribe's reservation boundaries rather than relying solely on jobs generated by the tribe's own enterprises.

Tribal economic development officials describe small-business ownership among tribal citizens as an area with significant room for growth, noting that many Cherokee Nation citizens interested in starting a business face financing barriers similar to those confronting small-business owners nationally, including difficulty securing traditional bank loans without an established credit history or collateral.

## Addressing Financing Gaps for Tribal Entrepreneurs

The expanded grant program is designed to provide capital that doesn't require the same collateral and credit history requirements as a conventional small-business loan, targeting citizens who have a viable business concept but limited access to traditional financing. Program administrators say grants are paired with technical assistance, including help with business planning and basic financial management, rather than simply providing funding without additional support.

Officials involved in administering the program say demand has grown steadily since it launched, with applications coming from a range of business types, including retail, food service, and professional services located both within larger towns like Tahlequah and in smaller reservation communities with fewer existing small-business options for residents.

## Building Long-Term Economic Capacity

Cherokee Nation officials describe the grant program as part of a longer-term strategy to diversify the tribe's economic base, complementing larger tribal enterprises with a broader ecosystem of citizen-owned small businesses that can create jobs and keep spending circulating within reservation communities rather than flowing to businesses located outside tribal boundaries entirely.

Program administrators say they track outcomes for grant recipients over time, including whether businesses remain operational and how many jobs they create, though officials caution that measuring long-term small-business success takes years rather than a single grant cycle. Tribal leaders have described continued investment in the program as a priority, citing entrepreneurship as one of several tools, alongside tribal enterprise growth and workforce development, aimed at strengthening the reservation's overall economy.
