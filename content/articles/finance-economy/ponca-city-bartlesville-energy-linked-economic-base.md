Ponca City and Bartlesville, two northern Oklahoma communities historically shaped by the energy industry, are pursuing somewhat different strategies to diversify their local economies as energy sector employment patterns continue to shift over time.

Both cities grew substantially around major energy companies in the twentieth century, and both have spent recent years working to reduce their economic dependence on a single dominant employer, even as the energy sector remains an important part of each city's economic base and identity.

## Two Communities, Two Approaches

Economic development officials in Ponca City describe an approach centered on attracting manufacturing and logistics employers who can take advantage of the city's existing industrial infrastructure and workforce experience, much of it originally built up to support energy sector operations. City leaders say the goal is to leverage the technical skills already present in the local workforce for a broader range of industrial employers, rather than assuming those skills are only transferable within the energy sector itself.

Bartlesville's economic development strategy has leaned more heavily on downtown revitalization and small-business growth, alongside continued investment in the city's arts and cultural amenities, an approach city officials describe as aimed at making the city an attractive place to live for remote workers and small-business owners who aren't necessarily tied to any single dominant employer.

## Shared Challenges Despite Different Strategies

Despite their different approaches, officials in both cities describe similar underlying challenges: an aging population in some cases, competition with larger metro areas for younger workers, and the ongoing need to convince employers unfamiliar with the region that a smaller northern Oklahoma city can offer a viable location for new investment. Both cities continue to benefit from a skilled technical workforce with roots in energy sector employment, a resource officials in both communities describe as a genuine competitive advantage even as they work to diversify beyond energy specifically.

Regional economists tracking both cities describe the diversification efforts as still a work in progress, with meaningful, if incremental, progress in reducing single-employer dependence, but acknowledge that energy sector health will likely remain an important factor in both local economies for the foreseeable future, regardless of diversification efforts underway.
