Several downtown Oklahoma City office buildings are being converted into residential units this year, as developers respond to a persistent gap between demand for downtown apartments and the number of employers still filling out pre-pandemic office footprints.

Commercial real estate brokers in the metro have described office vacancy rates that remain elevated compared with several years ago, even as downtown Oklahoma City's restaurant, retail, and entertainment scene has continued to draw steady foot traffic. That mismatch, between underused office space and strong interest in downtown living, has made conversion projects increasingly attractive to developers who might otherwise struggle to lease older office towers to traditional commercial tenants.

## The Economics of Converting Office to Residential

Converting an office building into residential units is rarely a straightforward or inexpensive process. Older office towers often have floor plates too deep to allow natural light into a standard apartment layout without significant structural changes, and plumbing systems designed for restrooms clustered on each floor need substantial rework to support kitchens and bathrooms in individual units. Developers involved in current downtown OKC projects have said the conversion math only works when acquisition costs for the aging building are low enough to offset the expensive retrofitting required.

City officials have shown interest in supporting conversion projects through targeted incentives, framing the effort as both an economic development priority and a practical response to buildings that would otherwise sit largely vacant. Proponents argue that adding residential density downtown supports existing restaurants and retailers by keeping more people living, not just working, in the urban core.

## What It Means for Downtown's Future

Real estate analysts tracking the trend caution that not every vacant office tower is a realistic conversion candidate, since building layout, structural condition, and location all affect whether the economics make sense. Even so, the projects currently underway represent a meaningful shift in how downtown Oklahoma City's building stock is being used, moving away from an office-dominated core toward a more mixed residential and commercial footprint.

Developers say leasing interest in the early conversion projects has been solid, particularly among younger professionals and empty-nesters drawn to a walkable downtown lifestyle. Whether the trend continues at scale will likely depend on interest rates, construction costs, and how quickly the remaining vacant office inventory downtown gets absorbed by either new tenants or additional conversion projects.
