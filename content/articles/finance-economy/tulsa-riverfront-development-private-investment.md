A new phase of mixed-use development along the Arkansas River in Tulsa is drawing fresh private investment, building on public riverfront improvements completed over the past several years that have reshaped how the city's waterfront is used.

Economic development officials describe a pattern that has played out in a number of mid-sized American cities: public investment in parks, trails, and riverfront infrastructure creates the conditions for private developers to justify residential and commercial projects nearby, betting that the improved public amenities will support demand for housing and retail space that wouldn't have penciled out along a less developed waterfront.

## Public Investment Setting the Stage

Tulsa's riverfront improvements, including expanded trail networks and public gathering spaces, were largely funded through a mix of public and philanthropic dollars over recent years, well ahead of the current wave of private development interest. City planning officials have said that sequencing, public investment first, private development second, was deliberate, intended to demonstrate the riverfront's potential before asking private developers to take on financial risk in the area.

The new development phase includes residential and retail components designed to take advantage of river views and proximity to the trail network, with developers citing strong early interest from prospective tenants and buyers drawn to the combination of urban amenities and outdoor recreation access that the riverfront now offers.

## Balancing Growth With Flood Considerations

Development along the Arkansas River requires careful attention to floodplain regulations, and project engineers have had to design buildings and site plans that account for periodic river level changes, a consideration that adds cost and complexity compared with development on higher, less flood-prone land elsewhere in the metro. City officials say those requirements are treated as a fixed cost of doing business along the riverfront rather than a negotiable point, given the practical and safety stakes involved.

Economic development officials describe the riverfront's continued growth as one of the more visible examples of how Tulsa has worked to diversify its economic base beyond its traditional energy sector roots, leaning into recreation, housing, and mixed-use development as complementary economic drivers rather than a replacement for the city's existing industries.
