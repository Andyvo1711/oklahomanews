Theo Banning began selling small-batch preserves and hot sauces at a weekend farmers market stand in Muskogee nine years ago, using produce sourced from his own small garden and a handful of neighboring growers. Today his company, Green Country Craft Foods, supplies its products to grocers across eastern Oklahoma, a growth trajectory Banning has attributed to slow, deliberate scaling rather than any single breakthrough moment.

Banning has said the transition from farmers market vendor to regional food supplier required rethinking nearly every part of the business, from sourcing enough consistent produce to meet grocery-scale demand to navigating food safety certifications that a market stand never required.

## Scaling Sourcing Without Losing Quality

One of the persistent challenges in moving from farmers market sales to grocery distribution, Banning has said, is maintaining consistent flavor and quality once volume increases beyond what a single small garden can supply. Green Country Craft Foods now sources produce from a network of small growers across eastern Oklahoma, a shift that required building relationships with multiple farms and establishing quality standards that didn't exist when Banning was simply using his own harvest.

Food safety certification proved to be another significant hurdle. Grocers require documented food safety practices and often specific certifications before agreeing to carry a small producer's products, a process Banning has described as slow and paperwork-heavy but ultimately necessary for any small food business hoping to move beyond direct-to-consumer sales at markets and festivals.

## Staying Small-Batch at a Larger Scale

Despite the growth, Banning has said maintaining a small-batch identity remains central to the brand, even as production volume has increased substantially from the company's farmers market origins. Green Country Craft Foods has invested in additional kitchen space and part-time staff to handle increased production, but Banning has resisted moving to fully automated, large-scale manufacturing processes that he believes would change the character of the products that built the brand's following in the first place.

Looking ahead, Banning has said he hopes to expand distribution to additional grocers across the wider region, while remaining cautious about growing faster than the company's sourcing network and quality standards can support. He has described the balance between growth and identity as an ongoing negotiation rather than a problem he expects to solve once and move past.
