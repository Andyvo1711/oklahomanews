Dana Whitfield started Meridian Air Components in a rented garage on the edge of Yukon eleven years ago with a single computer-controlled milling machine and a handful of contracts machining small parts for local repair shops. Today the company operates out of a 40,000-square-foot facility and holds certifications that let it supply precision components to several regional aerospace maintenance operations, part of a broader cluster of aviation-related manufacturing that has grown around the Oklahoma City metro's long history in aircraft maintenance and modification.

Whitfield, who trained as a machinist before starting the company, has described the early years as a matter of taking whatever precision work came through the door, whether it was aerospace-adjacent or not, in order to keep the shop's small crew employed while slowly building the quality certifications that larger aerospace customers require.

## Certification as the Real Barrier to Entry

Unlike many manufacturing sectors, aerospace supply chains require suppliers to hold specific quality certifications before a larger manufacturer will even consider a contract, a process that can take years and requires significant investment in documentation, testing equipment, and staff training. Whitfield has said navigating that certification process, more than any single piece of equipment, was the hardest part of moving the company from general-purpose machining into aerospace-specific work.

Meridian Air Components now holds certifications that allow it to supply structural and interior components to aerospace maintenance customers, and the company has added dedicated quality control staff to manage the documentation aerospace customers require for every batch of parts produced. Whitfield credits Oklahoma's existing base of aviation maintenance expertise, built up over decades around the region's aircraft depots, with giving a small supplier like Meridian a realistic path into the industry without needing to relocate closer to a coastal aerospace hub.

## Growth Plans Tied to Regional Aviation Demand

The company has added machinists and quality staff steadily over the past several years, and Whitfield has said future growth will likely track closely with overall demand from the region's aviation maintenance sector rather than any single large contract. Meridian has also begun exploring diversification into components for other precision manufacturing sectors, a hedge against the cyclical nature of aerospace demand that Whitfield has watched affect larger suppliers during past industry downturns.

For a company that started with one machine and a handful of local contracts, Whitfield describes the current scale as evidence that a disciplined, certification-first approach can work even for a small manufacturer without significant outside investment, provided the underlying regional demand for the work exists to support the slow climb.
