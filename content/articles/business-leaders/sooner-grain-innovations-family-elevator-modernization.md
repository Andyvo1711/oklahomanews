When Renata Castillo took over her family's grain elevator business near Stillwater eight years ago, the operation was running on much of the same equipment her grandfather had installed decades earlier. Today, Sooner Grain Innovations operates modernized storage and loading facilities that serve a wider network of area wheat farmers than the original elevator ever could, a transformation Castillo has described as necessary simply to keep the family business competitive.

Castillo, who studied agricultural business before returning to take over the family operation, has said the decision to invest heavily in modernization came from watching newer, larger grain facilities in the region pull business away from smaller, older elevators that couldn't offer the same efficient loading times or storage capacity.

## Competing Against Larger Regional Facilities

Grain elevators compete largely on speed and reliability during the narrow window of harvest season, when farmers need to unload trucks quickly to get back into the field. Older facilities with slower loading equipment can become a bottleneck that pushes farmers toward newer competitors, even those farther from home, simply because a faster unload means less time waiting in line during a season when every hour matters.

Castillo's modernization effort focused first on upgrading the elevator's loading and unloading equipment, followed by expanded storage capacity that lets the facility hold grain longer and market it more strategically rather than being forced to sell immediately after harvest simply due to space constraints. She has said the storage expansion, in particular, has given the business more flexibility in timing sales to farmers' advantage, a service smaller elevators without adequate storage typically can't offer.

## Balancing Tradition With Necessary Change

Castillo has described the modernization process as an emotional one at times, given the elevator's long history in her family, but has said her grandfather's original commitment to serving local farmers reliably remained the guiding principle even as nearly every piece of physical infrastructure changed. She has worked to maintain personal relationships with longtime farmer customers even as the business has scaled up its operations and added staff to manage the expanded facility.

Looking ahead, Castillo has said she expects continued investment in technology, including moisture-testing and grain-tracking systems, to remain necessary simply to keep pace with the storage and logistics capabilities that farmers now expect from any elevator they choose to do business with, regardless of its history or size.
