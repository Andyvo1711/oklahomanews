Cole Braddock spent over a decade working in the oilfield services industry near Woodward before pivoting to start Panhandle Wind Services, a turbine maintenance contractor that now serves several wind farms across western Oklahoma's expanding wind energy corridor.

Braddock has said the decision to move from oilfield work to wind turbine maintenance wasn't ideological but practical: he saw a growing number of wind farms being built across the region and recognized that many of the technical skills his former oilfield crew already had, from working at heights to troubleshooting complex mechanical and electrical systems, translated reasonably well to turbine maintenance work with additional specialized training.

## Retraining a Regional Workforce

Rather than recruiting an entirely new workforce from outside the region, Braddock built Panhandle Wind Services around retraining local workers who had experience in oilfield services or other technical trades. He has said this approach gave the company an early advantage in a competitive hiring market, since turbine maintenance requires specialized certifications that can take months to complete, but the underlying mechanical aptitude and comfort working at height were already present among his hires.

The company has partnered with equipment manufacturers to get technicians certified on specific turbine models, a process Braddock has described as ongoing rather than a one-time credential, since turbine technology continues to evolve and technicians need periodic recertification and additional training as new equipment comes online across the wind farms the company services.

## Navigating an Evolving Energy Landscape

Braddock has said one of the more difficult parts of running a company tied to wind energy has been navigating the broader uncertainty around energy policy and incentives that can affect how quickly new wind projects move forward in a given year. Rather than betting entirely on continued wind farm construction, Panhandle Wind Services has focused on maintenance contracts for existing turbines, a more stable revenue base than new construction work that depends heavily on project timelines outside the company's control.

Looking ahead, Braddock has said he expects the company's growth to track closely with how many turbines are actually operating across the region, rather than new construction announcements, and he has emphasized maintaining strong relationships with wind farm operators as the more reliable path to steady, long-term contracts.
