A primary care clinic in Ponca City has expanded its care coordination team to focus specifically on older adults managing multiple chronic conditions, an effort aimed at reducing hospitalizations linked to medication errors and missed follow-up appointments.

Older patients managing several chronic conditions at once, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and arthritis, often see multiple specialists in addition to their primary care provider, and the resulting mix of medications and follow-up instructions can become difficult to track without dedicated support. Clinic administrators say care coordination gaps, more than any single medical complication, have been a recurring driver of avoidable hospital visits among their older patient population.

## Untangling Complex Medication Regimens

The expanded coordination team includes staff dedicated to reviewing patients' full medication lists across all of their providers, checking for potentially dangerous interactions or duplications that can occur when different specialists prescribe medications without full visibility into what else a patient is taking. Pharmacists working with the clinic conduct periodic medication reviews with patients directly, walking through each prescription to confirm the patient understands dosing and potential side effects.

Clinic staff describe medication-related confusion as one of the more preventable causes of emergency room visits among older patients, particularly when a patient is taking medications from several different specialists who may not routinely communicate with one another. By centralizing medication review within the primary care clinic, the coordination team aims to catch problems before they result in a dangerous interaction or a missed dose that leads to a health crisis.

## Keeping Patients Connected to Follow-Up Care

Beyond medication management, the coordination team also tracks whether patients are keeping up with recommended follow-up appointments and lab work, reaching out proactively when a patient appears to have missed a scheduled visit. For older patients managing transportation challenges or cognitive changes that can make it harder to track a complex appointment schedule, that kind of proactive outreach can be the difference between catching a developing problem early and discovering it only after a hospitalization becomes necessary.

Clinic administrators say the coordination program is still relatively new, and they plan to track hospitalization rates among enrolled patients over the coming year to assess whether the added staff time translates into fewer avoidable emergency visits. For a community like Ponca City, where many older residents rely on a single local clinic for the bulk of their care, officials say the investment reflects a bet that better coordination, not additional medical technology, is the more immediate opportunity to improve outcomes.
