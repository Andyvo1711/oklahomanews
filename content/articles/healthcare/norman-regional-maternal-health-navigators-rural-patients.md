Norman Regional Health System has added a small team of maternal health navigators aimed at helping pregnant patients from smaller communities across central Oklahoma manage the logistics of prenatal care, from scheduling appointments to arranging transportation when a patient doesn't have a reliable vehicle.

The navigator role has become increasingly common at health systems trying to address maternal health disparities that show up starkly along geographic lines. Patients living in towns without a local obstetric provider often face long drives for routine prenatal visits, a burden that can lead some to skip appointments altogether, particularly if they're also juggling work schedules or childcare for other children.

## Filling Gaps Between Appointments

Navigators at Norman Regional work directly with patients between clinical visits, following up on missed appointments, helping arrange rides through community transportation programs, and answering basic questions that might otherwise go to an overburdened clinic scheduling line. The role is distinct from a nurse or midwife; navigators don't provide clinical care, but they handle the coordination work that can determine whether a patient actually makes it to the appointments her care team has scheduled.

Health system officials say the navigator program grew out of internal data showing that patients from more rural parts of central Oklahoma were more likely to have gaps in prenatal care compared with patients living closer to Norman itself. Rather than assuming those gaps reflected a lack of interest in prenatal care, the health system's maternal health team concluded that logistical barriers, not motivation, were driving much of the disparity.

## Early Feedback From Patients and Providers

Obstetric providers at Norman Regional say the navigators have already changed how some appointments unfold, since patients who might otherwise arrive stressed about transportation or confused about insurance paperwork now often have those issues resolved before they walk into the exam room. That, providers say, allows more of the visit itself to focus on the patient's actual pregnancy and health needs rather than administrative troubleshooting.

The health system plans to expand the navigator program gradually, adding staff as funding allows and tracking whether patients working with a navigator have fewer missed appointments and better overall engagement with prenatal care. For families in smaller central Oklahoma communities without a local obstetric provider, the program represents an attempt to bridge a distance that, for many patients, has been as much a barrier to care as anything happening inside the clinic itself.
