Green Country Yoga Collective in Tulsa has expanded its trauma-informed class offerings this year, training additional instructors in an approach designed to make yoga practice more accessible to students who may find traditional instructional cues, hands-on adjustments, or certain poses overwhelming due to past trauma.

Trauma-informed yoga instruction generally emphasizes offering students choices rather than direct commands, avoiding unsolicited physical adjustments from instructors, and using language that invites rather than instructs specific movements. Studio leadership say the approach grew out of requests from students who had found traditional yoga classes uncomfortable in ways that had little to do with physical flexibility or fitness level, and more to do with the classroom environment itself.

## Rethinking Instructional Language and Touch

Instructors trained in the trauma-informed approach describe a deliberate shift away from language that assumes every student wants the same physical experience in a pose, offering verbal options instead of a single prescribed form and explicitly telling students that any variation of a pose that feels appropriate to their own body is acceptable. Physical adjustments, once common in many yoga traditions as a way for instructors to help students align a pose correctly, are offered only with explicit prior consent under the studio's trauma-informed protocol, rather than applied by default during class.

Studio leadership say building this consent-based approach into every trauma-informed class has required consistent training and practice among instructors, since the shift represents a real change from teaching methods some instructors originally learned during their own certification training.

## Expanding Access Without Losing Rigor

Green Country Yoga Collective has been careful to describe the trauma-informed classes as still physically substantive practice, not a diluted version of yoga, emphasizing that the changes involve instructional approach and classroom environment rather than removing challenging poses or physical effort from the class itself. Students who have attended the trauma-informed sessions describe feeling more comfortable modifying poses without judgment, a shift instructors say has made some students willing to attend yoga classes regularly for the first time after previous negative experiences elsewhere.

The studio plans to continue expanding trauma-informed training among its instructor staff, and leadership say they've fielded interest from other regional studios curious about incorporating similar training into their own instructor development programs.
