A routine pedicure at Pro Nails & Spa turned into a troubling few days for one customer, according to her account. She describes a standard visit that included dead skin removal around the toenail, followed by pain, swelling, and eventually what she describes as pus in the days that followed. She also raises concerns about how tools appeared to be handled during her visit. This case touches on some of the most serious risks in nail care, and it is worth every salon reviewing its own practices in light of it.

## What the Client Says Happened During and After the Service

According to the client, a technician used nippers to remove dead skin near her toenail, and she felt some minor discomfort but did not notice bleeding or any clear injury at the time. It was only the following day that she noticed throbbing and redness, which she initially attributed to minor irritation. Over the next two days, she says the area became more swollen, and she noticed discharge while cleaning it, which led her to believe she had developed an infection. Aggressive skin or cuticle trimming during a pedicure, even without visible bleeding in the moment, can create a small opening that becomes a route for bacteria if the area isn't kept clean afterward. Technicians benefit from using a lighter touch around live skin, especially with nippers, and from letting clients know if any area was trimmed more closely than usual, so they can watch for symptoms and follow up with a provider if needed.

## Tool Handling and Why Sanitation Protocols Matter

The client also says she noticed tools being kept loose in a caddy rather than pulled from sealed packaging, which led her to suspect the salon's sanitation practices may not meet the standard needed to prevent infection. Proper sanitation, tools either single-use and sealed, or cleaned and sterilized between every client according to state board requirements, is one of the most important safety practices any nail salon can maintain, precisely because it is largely invisible to the customer unless something goes wrong. Salons should make their sanitation process visible where possible, whether that means visibly opening sealed tool packages in front of clients or clearly displaying an autoclave and sterilization log, so customers have confidence in what they can't otherwise see for themselves.

## Editorial Perspective

A few lessons stand out from this account. First, aggressive trimming near live skin carries real risk even without visible bleeding, and a lighter, more cautious approach protects both the client and the salon. Second, visible, consistent sanitation practices are not optional; they are foundational to client safety and should be something every technician can demonstrate without hesitation. Third, when a client reports a possible infection following a service, the priority for any salon should be genuine concern and encouragement to seek medical care promptly, alongside a serious internal review of tool handling procedures.
