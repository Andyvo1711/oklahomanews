Sometimes the service is lovely and one small step undoes the whole impression. This report on Lux Nail Bar is a good example: the client genuinely enjoyed her pedicure, and it was the finish, plus how the fix was handled, that left her frustrated. Both parts are easy to get right, which is what makes the case worth a look.

## A Great Pedicure, and Polish That Wouldn't Set

The client was happy with the $52 pedicure itself. The trouble was the regular polish. She sat under the fan for 25 to 30 minutes before leaving, and about an hour later, roughly an hour and a half after it was applied, she touched her big toe and the color smeared. Looking at her photos afterward, she also felt the original polish had gone on streaky. Polish that is still soft well over an hour after application usually points to something in the technique or the product, such as coats applied too thick or not given enough time, rather than anything the client did. Getting a smooth, fully set finish is a core part of what a pedicure is meant to deliver.

## Charged to Fix the Salon's Own Issue

When she came back, the technician touched another toe and it smeared as well. The salon offered to repaint the two affected toes, but understandably she preferred gel to avoid the same thing happening again, and she was then charged $15 for it. This is the part that stayed with her. As she put it, she should not have been charged for something that was not her fault, and she felt taken advantage of for being easygoing about it. When a finish fails this soon, the fix, whatever form it takes, is really the salon's to cover. Asking the client to pay extra to solve a problem she did not create turns a simple redo into a reason to feel let down.

## Editorial Perspective

The good news is that both halves of this are simple to fix. First, focus on a finish that lasts: thin, even coats, enough dry time before a client leaves, and a quick check that the polish is truly set can prevent smearing and streaks entirely. If regular polish is prone to this on a busy day, gently suggesting a longer-wearing option up front, at no surprise cost later, sets the right expectation. Second, stand behind the work. When a service fails within the hour, the redo should be free, and if a client reasonably chooses an upgrade like gel to avoid a repeat, waiving or absorbing that charge is what turns a frustrated visit back into a loyal customer. This client openly loved her pedicure and did not want to complain. Covering the small cost to make the polish right would have cost far less than the goodwill it lost, and it is exactly the gesture that brings an easygoing, happy client back again.
