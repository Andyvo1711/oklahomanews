A mother brought her seven-year-old daughter to Luscious Nail & Spa for a shared outing: a pedicure and acrylics for herself, and a set for her child. According to her review, she left unhappy with the shape of both sets, the way staff responded when she raised concerns, and the final bill. Her experience touches three areas every salon can learn from: technical consistency, how we talk about a client's natural nails, and how we price services for children.

## Uneven Shaping and a Missing Apex

The client described her own acrylics as noticeably crooked, and said her daughter's nails had "no apex," with a surface she compared to a mountain slope. The apex is the structural high point of an acrylic enhancement. It gives the nail strength and a balanced look, and when it is missing or misplaced, the set tends to look flat, uneven, or lopsided from the side.

Consistent shaping comes from habit, not luck. Technicians should check each nail from the front, the side, and down the length of the finger before moving on, and compare all ten nails together before the client leaves the chair. For small or young hands, the product should be applied in thinner, more controlled layers, since there is less nail surface to work with. A quick final check with the client, holding her hands up and asking whether the shape looks right, gives the salon a chance to fix small issues on the spot rather than reading about them later.

## Placing the Blame on the Client's Nails

The part of the visit that seemed to frustrate the client most was the explanation she received. In her account, staff repeatedly said the results were due to her having "small nail beds." Whether or not nail bed size affected the work, hearing it framed as the client's fault left her feeling dismissed, and she described the team as rude.

Every client's nails are different, and a skilled technician adapts to them. If a client's natural nail shape does limit what is possible, that conversation belongs at the start of the service, framed as a recommendation: "Your nail beds are on the shorter side, so I'd suggest this length and shape for the best result." Said after the fact, the same information sounds like an excuse. Staff should also avoid repeating a point once a client is upset. One calm acknowledgment, followed by an offer to adjust or redo the nails, does far more to protect the relationship.

## Pricing a Child's Service

The client was charged $105 for her pedicure and $50 for her daughter's nails. She noted that she usually pays between $50 and $80 for her own long acrylic set, so paying $50 for a child's set felt unreasonable to her.

Children's services are a sensitive area, and families notice when pricing does not seem to reflect a smaller, simpler service. Salons benefit from a clear, posted kids' menu with defined prices, and from confirming the total with the parent before starting. Many salons also choose to steer young children toward gentler options such as polish or press-ons, which are better suited to growing nails and easier for parents to feel good about. Transparent pricing prevents the surprise at checkout that so often turns into a negative review.

## Editorial Perspective

This visit ended with the client saying she would tell people she knows to stay away, which shows how quickly one appointment can affect a salon's reputation in its community. First, build a consistent quality check into every acrylic service, especially the apex and overall symmetry, and invite the client to review the shape before she leaves. Second, discuss any limits of a client's natural nails before the service begins, and never use them to explain away a result afterward. Third, keep a clear, fair menu for children's services and confirm prices with parents up front. Families who feel respected and fairly charged become some of a salon's most loyal regulars.
