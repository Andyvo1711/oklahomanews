Employers across the Oklahoma City metro are expanding workplace wellness offerings to include mental health resources, flexible scheduling support, and stress management programming, moving beyond the gym membership discounts and step-count challenges that once defined typical corporate wellness benefits.

Human resources professionals in the region describe growing employee demand for wellness benefits that address mental health and work-life balance directly, rather than programs focused narrowly on physical fitness incentives. Several area employers have added expanded mental health counseling benefits, on-site or virtual therapy access, and manager training focused on recognizing signs of employee burnout as part of a broader wellness benefits overhaul.

## Moving Beyond the Gym Discount Model

HR professionals describe traditional wellness programs, often centered on gym membership discounts or wellness point systems tied to step counts and biometric screenings, as increasingly viewed by employees as disconnected from their actual day-to-day stressors, which more often relate to workload, scheduling flexibility, and mental health support than physical fitness access specifically. Employers making changes say they've shifted program design based directly on employee survey feedback, rather than continuing to invest in wellness benefits employees weren't using or valuing.

Flexible scheduling has emerged as one of the more consistently requested wellness benefits, with employees describing the ability to adjust work hours around family needs or personal health appointments as more valuable to their overall well-being than traditional fitness perks. Several employers have implemented formal flexible scheduling policies specifically as part of their wellness benefits packaging, rather than treating scheduling flexibility as a separate HR policy issue.

## Measuring Success Differently

HR professionals say measuring the success of these newer wellness benefits has required different metrics than the participation rates and biometric outcomes typically used to evaluate traditional fitness-focused programs. Employee engagement surveys, retention data, and utilization rates for mental health benefits have become more central to how employers assess whether their wellness investments are working, a shift some HR leaders describe as still evolving as more employers experiment with these program changes.

Employers making these changes describe the shift as driven partly by broader cultural conversations about mental health and partly by practical retention concerns in a competitive metro-area labor market, where wellness benefits increasingly factor into how prospective employees evaluate job offers alongside salary and traditional benefits like health insurance.
