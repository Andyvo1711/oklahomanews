A grandmother and her granddaughter visited Sunny Nail & Spa together for manicures and pedicures, the kind of shared outing that can turn a family into long-term regulars. Instead, according to the review, both left "so disappointed." The client described a mix-up over what "gel" meant, nails that chipped and looked uneven, and a combined bill of $263 that did not feel matched to the results. Her experience is a useful reminder that clear communication matters just as much as technique.

## When "Gel" Means Different Things

The client explained that her nails were supposed to be gel, but what she received felt to her like gel polish, while she was still charged for gel. A few days later, she said her nails were "actually bending" because they felt so weak.

This is one of the most common sources of confusion in the industry. To many clients, "gel" means a structured overlay, such as builder gel or hard gel, that adds strength to the natural nail. To many salons, "gel" simply means gel polish, which adds color and shine but very little strength. Both are valid services, but they are not the same, and they are usually priced differently.

The fix starts at the front desk and continues at the station. Staff should ask a simple question before starting: "Are you looking for gel color only, or a gel overlay to strengthen your nails?" The menu should use clear names for each option, with a short description and price for each. If a client has thin or flexible natural nails, the technician can recommend an overlay and explain why. That short conversation prevents the feeling of paying for one thing and receiving another.

## Uneven Application and Early Chipping

The granddaughter's nails, according to the review, came out uneven and chipped. Chipping soon after a gel service usually points to preparation or application steps, such as incomplete cuticle cleanup, oils left on the nail plate, skipped or thin base coat, product flooding onto the skin, or free edges that were not capped.

Technicians can build a consistent routine around these steps: thorough prep and dehydration, thin even layers, capping the free edge with every coat, and full curing time under the lamp. Before the client leaves, a final check of each nail under good lighting catches thick spots, uneven lines, and edges that may lift. Offering a short fix period, where clients can return for a touch-up if a nail chips within the first few days, also turns a disappointing result into a chance to rebuild trust.

## Making the Bill Feel Fair

The client paid $263 for two mani-pedi services and felt the price did not reflect what they received. When the service itself is in question, the total on the receipt becomes the focal point of the complaint.

Salons can reduce this friction by confirming the service and estimated total with the client before work begins, especially for group or family visits where add-ons add up quickly. An itemized receipt helps clients see exactly what they paid for. If a client raises a concern at checkout, a manager should listen, check the nails, and offer a redo or adjustment rather than letting the client leave unhappy.

## Editorial Perspective

This visit brought in two clients from the same family, which makes getting it right even more important. First, define "gel" clearly on the menu and confirm with every client whether she wants gel color or a strengthening overlay. Second, follow a consistent prep and application routine, and check every nail before the client leaves the chair. Third, confirm prices up front for group visits and give clients an easy way to return for a fix. Families who feel heard and fairly charged are far more likely to book their next outing together.
